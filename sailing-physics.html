Sailing Physics

How Valheim's ships actually move — decompiled from the game itself, and checked against real sailing logs.

Mass doesn't matter

It's tempting to assume a heavier ship is slower to get moving, or slower at full sail. It isn't. Every force the game applies to a ship — buoyancy, the rudder, the sail — is applied through Unity's Rigidbody.AddForceAtPosition(..., ForceMode.Impulse), and an impulse divides by mass to produce a velocity change. Valheim's code pre-multiplies each force by the ship's own mass right before applying it:

m_body.AddForceAtPosition(sailForce * m_body.mass, position2, ForceMode.Impulse);

Multiply by mass, then Unity divides by that same mass — the two cancel, every physics tick. A Karve and a Drakkar under the same sail settings pick up speed at exactly the same rate. This is true of buoyancy and rudder-turning forces too, not just the sail.

Drag works differently, and it's worth knowing why it doesn't reintroduce mass. The forward-drag term is a straight subtraction from velocity — no AddForce, no mass anywhere in it:

float num8 = num5 * num5 * Sign(num5) * m_dampingForward * num4;   // num5 = forward speed
vector9 -= transform.forward * Clamp(num8, -1f, 1f);                // subtracted straight from velocity

So acceleration, top speed and turning are all governed purely by the ship's m_sailForceFactor and m_dampingForward — mass only matters for what happens when a ship hits something.

The wind-angle formula

Decompiling Ship.GetWindAngleFactor() and Ship.GetSailForce() (assembly_valheim.dll, ilspycmd) gives the exact curve the game uses to turn wind direction into thrust:

public float GetWindAngleFactor()
{
    float num = Vector3.Dot(EnvMan.instance.GetWindDir(), -transform.forward);
    float num2 = Mathf.Lerp(0.7f, 1f, 1f - Abs(num));   // 0.7 astern/ahead -> 1.0 abeam
    float num3 = 1f - Utils.LerpStep(0.75f, 0.8f, num); // hard cutoff near dead upwind
    return num2 * num3;
}

private Vector3 GetSailForce(float sailSize, float dt)
{
    float windIntensity = EnvMan.instance.GetWindIntensity();
    float num = Mathf.Lerp(0.25f, 1f, windIntensity);          // weak wind still pushes a little
    float windAngleFactor = GetWindAngleFactor() * num;
    Vector3 target = Normalize(windDir + transform.forward) * (windAngleFactor * m_sailForceFactor * sailSize);
    ...
}

Two things are stacked here, and they pull in different directions:

  • windAngleFactor alone peaks at 1.0 with the wind on the beam (90° to your heading) and bottoms out at 0.7 running dead downwind or sailing dead into the wind.
  • The thrust direction isn't straight forward — it's the average (bisector) of the wind direction and the ship's heading, Normalize(windDir + forward). Only the forward-facing slice of that bisector actually moves the ship, and that slice shrinks the further the wind angle is from dead-astern, following cos(angle / 2).

Multiply the two together and the peak moves. It isn't at the beam, and it isn't running downwind — it lands at a broad reach, angled off the stern.

Drive across every point of sail

Combined drive = windAngleFactor(θ) × cos(θ / 2), where θ is the wind angle measured off the stern (0° = wind dead astern / running downwind, 180° = wind dead ahead). This is exactly what's plotted below, computed live from the formulas above.

Peak drive (0.737) lands at 64° off the stern — a broad reach. The shaded band past ~139° is the "in irons" zone: no forward thrust at all within about 37° of sailing straight into the wind.

Is that realistic?

The shape is — dead-downwind not being the fastest point of sail is genuinely true of real sailboats. The specific number (64°) is a hand-tuned game curve, though, not a physics derivation, so the resemblance is qualitative rather than exact.

Why real boats work out this way:

  • Apparent wind. A moving boat's own velocity combines with the true wind to produce the "apparent wind" the sail actually feels. On a reach, that apparent wind is stronger and hits the sail at a better angle than on a dead run.
  • Lift vs. drag. Reaching lets a sail generate lift, like an airfoil — efficient. Running dead downwind is pure drag propulsion, and a boat running dead downwind can't really exceed the true wind speed itself.
  • Rig efficiency. Dead downwind, a mainsail often blankets the headsail, and you risk sailing "by the lee" — genuinely worse trim, not just a geometric artifact.

In real sailing convention (true wind angle measured from the bow, 0° = head to wind, 180° = dead run), Valheim's "64° off the stern" converts to 180° − 64° = 116° true wind angle — squarely in broad-reach territory, a very plausible number for a cruising boat. High-performance boats (catamarans, foilers) generate so much of their own apparent wind that they can peak even further forward, closer to a beam reach.

What the game's model doesn't do: it only ever uses the true wind direction — there's no feedback from the ship's own speed into an apparent wind angle, no lift/drag distinction, no heel or leeway. The 64° peak was almost certainly tuned by feel, not simulated — it just happens to land in the right real-world neighborhood.

Turning this into real numbers

At steady state, the sail's forward push balances the forward drag term exactly, which gives a clean formula for top speed at any wind angle:

topSpeed(θ) = sqrt( drive(θ) × sailForceFactor / dampingForward )

Rather than trust that formula blind, it's checked against real gameplay: the Captain's Log mod logs speed, heading, wind angle and wind intensity twice a second while sailing. Every steady full-sail stretch (speed and drive both flat for a few consecutive samples) is a data point, fit to speed² = k × drive by linear regression through the origin — and the top speed at the best angle is √(k × 0.737).
